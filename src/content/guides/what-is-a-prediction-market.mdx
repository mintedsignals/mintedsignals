A prediction market is a place where people buy and sell shares in the outcome of a future event, such as an election, a central bank decision or a match result. Each share pays $1 if the outcome happens and nothing if it doesn't, so the price of a share, between 0 and 100 cents, works as the crowd's estimate of how likely that outcome is.

## How YES and NO shares work

Most prediction markets ask a yes-or-no question: *Will the Fed cut rates at its next meeting?* You can buy one of two kinds of share:

- **YES shares** pay $1 if the answer turns out to be yes.
- **NO shares** pay $1 if the answer turns out to be no.

Because exactly one of the two will pay out, a YES share and a NO share together are always worth $1. If YES trades at 64¢, NO trades at roughly 36¢.

You don't have to hold a share until the end. Prices move as news arrives, and you can sell at the current price at any time while the market is open.

## Why prices act like probabilities

Suppose YES trades at 64¢. If you think the true chance is higher than 64%, buying YES is a good bet for you. If you think it's lower, you would rather buy NO. People with different views keep trading until the price settles where buyers and sellers balance out.

That's why a price of 64¢ is usually read as **"the market thinks there's about a 64% chance."** It's not a guarantee, and markets can be wrong, but the price sums up the money-weighted opinion of everyone trading.

## What happens when the market ends

When the event is over, the market **resolves**: the venue checks the result against the rules written for that market and pays $1 per winning share. Losing shares become worthless. Each market's rules say exactly which source decides the outcome. Our guide on [how prediction markets resolve](/guides/how-prediction-markets-resolve) covers this in detail, including what happens when a result is disputed.

## Markets with more than two outcomes

Some questions have many possible answers, such as *Who will win the election?* Venues usually handle these as a group of yes-or-no markets, one per candidate. Each candidate's YES price is that candidate's implied chance, and the prices across the group should add up to roughly 100%.

## Where you can trade

The two largest venues today are Polymarket and Kalshi. They work differently: Polymarket runs an on-chain order book and settles in USDC, while Kalshi is a CFTC-regulated exchange that settles in US dollars. Who can use each depends on where you live. See [Polymarket vs Kalshi](/guides/polymarket-vs-kalshi) and [are prediction markets legal where you live?](/guides/are-prediction-markets-legal)

## Key points

- A share pays $1 if its outcome happens, $0 if not.
- The price in cents reads as the market's implied chance in percent.
- You can buy or sell any time before the market closes.
- Trading involves risk of loss. Odds are information, not financial advice.
