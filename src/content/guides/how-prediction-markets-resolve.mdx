A prediction market resolves when the event it's about is over and the venue declares which outcome happened. Every market has written rules that name the question, the deadline and the source that decides the answer, such as an official election result or a central bank statement. Winning shares then pay $1 each and losing shares pay nothing.

## Read the rules, not just the question

The headline question is a summary. The rules are what count. They usually say:

- **What exactly has to happen**: for example "a cut of 25 basis points or more", not just "a rate cut".
- **The deadline**: what happens if the event occurs after the market closes.
- **The resolution source**: which publication, agency or data feed is used.
- **Edge cases**: what happens if the event is cancelled, postponed or the source is unavailable.

## When results are unclear

Most markets resolve without any question. Problems come up when:

- **The wording doesn't fit what happened.** For example, an announcement is made but not formally confirmed.
- **The source changes or disappears.**
- **The event is cancelled or delayed past the deadline.**

In these cases the rules decide, and where they're silent each venue has its own process. <Check>Add each venue's documented approach for ambiguous outcomes, including any 50/50 or refund rules</Check>

## Markets that close early

Some markets close as soon as the outcome is certain, for example once a candidate concedes. Others stay open until a fixed date. The rules say which.

## What this means for you

- Read the rules before trading, especially on close or unusual events.
- Prices near 0¢ or 100¢ late in a market often reflect an outcome that's already known but not yet settled.
- Disputes can delay payouts.
